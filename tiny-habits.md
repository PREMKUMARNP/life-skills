### Question 1: What was the most interesting story or idea for you?
The idea I liked most is that a habit should be so small that it feels almost silly not to do it. For example, instead of "I will exercise for an hour," you start with "I will do two push-ups." I found this very true for me. I always make big plans like studying 3 hours a day, and after two or three days I give up. But if I tell myself "just open the book and read one page," it feels easy. Most of the time I end up studying more than one page because starting is the hardest part.

### Question 2: How can you use B = MAP to make making new habits easier?  What are M, A and P?

B = MAP means a Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt all come together at the same moment.
M (Motivation): how much I want to do it.
A (Ability): how easy it is for me to do it.
P (Prompt): the reminder that tells me to do it right now.

Motivation is not stable. Some days I feel like studying and some days I don't. So the better way is to make the habit really easy and connect it to something I already do. For example: "After I brush my teeth at night, I will read one page of a book." Brushing is the prompt, and one page is so easy that I don't need much motivation. Once it becomes normal, I can slowly increase it.

### Question 3: Why it is important to "shine" or celebrate after each successful completion of habit?

Celebrating is important because it gives our brain a good feeling right after the habit. Our brain likes to repeat things that feel good, so the habit slowly becomes automatic. The celebration doesn't need to be big. It can be a smile, a fist pump, or saying "Yes, I did it!" to myself. It may feel awkward in the beginning, but it works. If I finish my study session and feel proud, I'll look forward to doing it again tomorrow. If I finish it and feel nothing, it just stays a boring task. That's why whatever we celebrate becomes a habit.

### Question 4: In this video, what was the most interesting story or idea for you?

What I found interesting is that tiny improvements add up to something big. If I get just 1% better every day, in one year I will be many times better than I am now. It also works the other way. Small bad habits, like sleeping late or wasting 30 minutes on my phone daily, slowly pull me down without me noticing. This made me realize I don't need a huge change in my life. If I just improve a little each day, like learning one new thing or revising for 10 extra minutes, the results will show after a few months.

### Question 5: what is the book's perspective about identity?

According to Atomic Habits, real change starts when we change who we think we are. Most people only think about goals, like "I want to get good marks." The book says we should think about identity instead, like "I am a hard-working student." Every small action is like a vote for that identity. When I study even for 10 minutes, I'm proving to myself that I'm that kind of person. For example, saying "I don't smoke" is stronger than "I'm trying to quit smoking." When our habits match our identity, we don't have to force ourselves.

### Question 6: write about the book's perspective on how to make a habit easier to do?

The book gives four simple ideas:

Make it obvious: Keep the cue where I can see it. If I want to read, I keep the book on my study table. If I want to drink more water, I keep a bottle next to me.
Make it attractive: Link the habit with something I enjoy, like listening to my favourite songs only while studying.
Make it easy: Reduce the effort. I can keep my gym clothes ready the night before. I can also use the two-minute rule, which means starting with just two minutes of the habit.
Make it satisfying: Give myself a small reward or tick off the day on a calendar so I can see my progress.

### Question 7:  write about the book's perspective on how to make a habit harder to do?

For bad habits, the book says to do the opposite:

Make it invisible: Remove the cue. If I waste too much time on Instagram, I can delete the app or keep my phone in another room.
Make it unattractive: Remind myself of the bad results, like wasted time, tiredness or low marks.
Make it difficult: Add extra steps. For example, I can log out of all apps so I have to enter my password every time.
Make it unsatisfying: Tell a friend about my goal so that I feel accountable. If I fail, I will feel a little embarrassed, and that helps me stop.

### Question 8: Pick one habit that you would like to do more of? What are the steps that you can take to make it make the cue obvious or the habit more attractive or easy and or response satisfying?

I want to revise my notes for at least 10 minutes every day. Here is how I will do it:

Make the cue obvious: Keep my notebook on my study table and set an alarm for 8 PM. I will also link it to something I already do: "After I finish dinner, I will open my notes."
Make it easy: Start with just 2 minutes or one page, so there is no pressure.
Make it attractive: Sit in a nice spot with my favourite playlist or a cup of tea.
Make it satisfying: Tick off the day on a habit tracker and say "Yes!" after finishing. At the end of a good week, I can treat myself to a movie or my favourite snack.

### Question 9: Pick one habit that you would like to eliminate or do less of? What are the steps that you can take to make it make the cue invisible or the process unattractive or hard or the response unsatisfying?

I want to cut down on scrolling reels on my phone late at night. Here is my plan:

Make the cue invisible: Charge my phone away from my bed and turn off notifications, so nothing pulls me in.
Make it unattractive: Remind myself that scrolling makes me sleep late, feel tired in the morning and waste my day.
Make it hard: Set a 30-minute app timer, log out of social media, or move the apps off my home screen.
Make it unsatisfying: Tell a friend or roommate my goal and ask them to check on me. I won't want to admit that I broke it.
